Space · Earth Observation — Data Acquisition

Earth
Observation

Root question: how do you learn what's on the ground from 500 km up, without touching it?

Based on "The Anatomy of Earth Observation" · 10 Jan 2025

The axioms

  1. Everything you can know arrives as electromagnetic energy.A satellite never touches the ground. It only receives waves: sunlight the surface reflected, heat the surface emitted, or an echo of a pulse the satellite sent itself.
  2. Who supplies the energy splits all sensors in two.Passive sensors borrow energy from the Sun or Earth, so they are cheap but depend on daylight and weather. Active sensors bring their own, so they work day, night and through cloud, at higher cost.
  3. Every material has a spectral fingerprint.Healthy leaves reflect near-infrared strongly, water absorbs it, minerals leave absorption dips in shortwave IR. Measure more wavelengths and you can tell more materials apart.
  4. Sharpness is capped by diffraction: detail ≈ 1.22 · λ · altitude / aperture.Short wavelengths or big mirrors give fine detail. This one formula explains why optical satellites reach 30 cm and why radar needs a trick.
  5. Long waves penetrate, short waves resolve.Microwaves pass through clouds, smoke and some canopy but see coarser detail. Visible light sees fine detail but stops at the first cloud. Every sensor choice is a position on this trade.

Why radar has to fake a giant antenna

Axiom 4 is where things get interesting. Try the calculator: an optical telescope with a 1.1 m mirror resolves about 30 cm. Switch to a 3 cm radar wave and the same physics asks for an antenna kilometres long.

The SAR trick. A satellite moving at ~7.5 km/s records echoes from the same spot over a few hundred metres of its path, then combines them in software as if they came from one antenna that long. Along-track detail ends up near D/2, half the physical antenna length, regardless of altitude. That's the "synthetic aperture".

The InSAR trick. The phase of a radar echo tells you the path length to within a small fraction of the wavelength. Compare two passes and a phase shift reveals ground movement of millimetres to centimetres: subsidence, glacier flow, a bridge settling.

From photons to money

Why constellations win

One big satellite passes over a spot every few days. Dozens of small ones working together can pass every few hours. For a flood, a crop outbreak or a ship, how recently you looked matters more than how sharply. The holy-grails essay sets the bar at revisit under 15 minutes, with AI processing images on the satellite so only answers come down.

Indian players named in the post

  • ISRO — operates India's EO fleet across optical and radar.
  • Pixxel — hyperspectral. Shakuntala launched April 2022; "Fireflies" constellation announced.
  • GalaxEye and PierSight — SAR payloads flown on PSLV-C60's POEM-4 module.

Mental model

A satellite is a remote question about a wavelength. Choose the wavelength by what you need to see through, and the sensor by what property of the wave you'll measure.

Defence · Part 1 — Autonomous Systems

Autonomous
Systems

Root question: what does a machine need to fight without a person inside, and how do you stop one?

Based on "The Anatomy of Defence Tech in India — Part 1" · 9 Feb 2025

The axioms

  1. Every unmanned system is four subsystems.A body that moves (energy and propulsion), a sense of where it is (navigation), a link to its humans (communications), and a job (payload: camera, jammer, warhead, cargo).
  2. Autonomy means moving decisions from the link into the body.A remote-piloted drone dies when the link is jammed. An autonomous one keeps going. The more contested the spectrum, the more valuable onboard decision-making becomes.
  3. Cost exchange decides wars of attrition.If a cheap attacker forces an expensive response, the defender runs out of money or interceptors first. The Nagorno-Karabakh and Ukraine wars pushed militaries toward small, expendable drones for this reason.
  4. Each of the four subsystems is an attack surface.Counter-drone isn't one technology. It's a menu: jam the link, spoof the navigation, blind the sensor, or physically break the body.
  5. The medium sets the hardest problem.In air, moving is easy and hiding is hard. On land, terrain makes perception hardest. Under water, radio dies within metres, so machines have to be autonomous because nobody can steer them.

The threat picture

Cross-border drones smuggle weapons and narcotics, a 2021 drone attack hit the Jammu Air Force Station, and China has tested autonomous swarms. The post's point: the future battlefield will be shaped by who can neutralise drones as much as who owns them.

The policy scaffolding

Mental model

Energy · Fission, fusion and India

Nuclear
Energy

Root question: where does nuclear energy come from, and why is it so hard to harvest?

Based on "The Anatomy of Nuclear Energy" by Sanket & Sanjiv · 19 Apr 2025

The axioms

  1. A nucleus weighs less than the parts it's made of.The missing mass is binding energy (E = mc²). Rearranging nuclei into more tightly bound forms turns some mass into energy.
  2. Binding peaks at iron and nickel.Moving toward the peak from either side releases energy. Split heavy nuclei (fission) or fuse light ones (fusion). Same hill, opposite slopes.
  3. Nuclear bonds are about a million times stronger than chemical ones.Burning a carbon atom releases ~4 eV. Splitting a uranium atom releases ~200 MeV. That's why one fingertip pellet matches 1,780 lb of coal.
  4. Releasing the energy is easy. Controlling it and moving the heat out is the whole engineering problem.Fission needs a chain reaction held exactly at balance. Fusion needs fuel hotter than the Sun's core held together long enough to burn.
  5. A reactor is a heat source; temperature is the product.Hotter coolant means more electricity per unit of heat, and access to industrial uses like hydrogen and steel. Coolant choice sets the temperature.

The hill both reactions climb

Fission: holding a chain reaction at exactly 1

Each U-235 fission releases 2–3 neutrons. If on average exactly one of them causes another fission, power is steady. More than one and power climbs; fewer and it dies. Every reactor has the same four parts:

  • Fuel — uranium, plutonium or thorium-bred U-233.
  • Moderator — slows neutrons so U-235 catches them (water, graphite). Fast reactors skip it.
  • Coolant — carries heat out. Sets the outlet temperature.
  • Control — absorbs spare neutrons to hold the balance.

Why go small?

Twentieth-century reactors were gigawatt-scale, custom-built on site, and took 10–15 years. Small Modular Reactors (under 300 MWe) and microreactors (around 20 MWe) are built in factories and assembled on site.

The bet is the same learning curve that made aircraft and solar panels cheap: build the same unit many times. Smaller cores can also be cooled by gravity and natural heat radiation in an emergency, with no pumps or operators needed.

Fusion: three knobs, one finish line

To fuse, deuterium and tritium nuclei must collide fast enough to overcome their electric repulsion. That means temperatures above 100 million °C. The Lawson criterion says you need enough density (n), held for enough time (τ), at that temperature, to get more energy out than you put in. Every fusion approach is a different bet on which knob to turn.

The unlock: stronger magnets

At fixed plasma pressure ratio, fusion power density scales roughly with the fourth power of magnetic field. Double the field, get ~16× the power in the same volume, or shrink the machine. High-temperature superconducting (HTS) magnets did exactly this, which is what makes compact tokamaks and stellarators viable. Better simulation did the rest for stellarator coil design.

Is waste really the problem?

Sixty years of US commercial nuclear produced ~90,000 t of spent fuel, enough to cover one football field about 10 m high. Fossil fuels emit ~36 billion t of CO₂ every year. Spent fuel still holds over 90% of its energy. Fast reactors can burn it and cut its hazardous life from ~100,000 years to a few hundred.

India's three-stage plan, from first principles

India has little uranium and a large share of the world's thorium. The catch: thorium can't sustain a chain reaction by itself. It has to be converted into U-233 by absorbing neutrons. Homi Bhabha's three stages exist to manufacture those neutrons.

Why now. Data centres and AI need firm, clean power. Solar and wind can't carry a grid alone. A 1% rise in GDP per capita drives ~0.6% more energy use. The post's thesis: reactors manufactured like aerospace systems, not built like dams, on 2–3 year timelines, sized to plug into a data centre or decarbonise steel and ammonia. What India lacks is a framework for entrepreneurial risk in nuclear: regulatory sandboxes, capital pathways and a public story beyond the Cold War.

Mental model

Nuclear power is climbing toward iron on the binding-energy hill. Fission climbs down from uranium, fusion climbs up from hydrogen, and all the engineering is about controlling the pace and catching the heat.

Defence · Network-centric warfare, software and AI

Software-
Defined War

Root question: if two sides have similar weapons, what decides who wins? The speed at which each can see, decide and act.

Combines "The Anatomy of Network Centric Warfare" (26 Jul 2025) and "The Anatomy of Software and AI in Defence" (11 Aug 2025)

The axioms

  1. Combat is a race of decision loops.Observe, Orient, Decide, Act (OODA). The side that completes its loop faster acts on reality while the other is still reacting to what already happened.
  2. Information decays.A target position from ten minutes ago is worthless for a moving target. The value of a sensor is its data multiplied by how fast that data reaches a shooter.
  3. Bespoke integration grows as n². Standards grow as n.Connecting n systems pairwise needs n(n−1)/2 custom links. A shared standard needs n adapters. This is the arithmetic behind MOSA.
  4. Hardware changes in years, software in days.If a capability lives in metal, its upgrade cycle is a procurement cycle. If it lives in code, it can change overnight, like a phone update.
  5. Links get jammed, so intelligence must live at the edge.A drone or outpost cut off from the network must still detect, classify and act on its own. Centralised analysis is a single point of failure.

Sensor to shooter, across domains

Treat AI like a weapon

The pipeline

  • Ingest — satellite imagery, radar, ELINT, SIGINT, HUMINT.
  • Fuse — cross-check sources, drop false positives, score confidence.
  • Predict — likely enemy movement, ambush sites, anomalies.
  • Recommend — which shooter, which countermeasure.
  • Human decides — trained to read confidence scores and spot spoofing.

Every fielded model needs

  • Provenance — where the training data came from, known biases.
  • Version control — instant rollback if a flaw appears.
  • Adversarial testing — spoofed, degraded and jammed inputs.
  • Explainability — operators see why, and can override.
  • Sovereign training — air-gapped; federated learning keeps raw data in secure domains.

Priority use cases for India, according to the post: target recognition from EO/IR and SAR, real-time ELINT classification, maritime anomaly detection across the Indian Ocean Region, counter-swarm tracking, and adaptive EW that tunes jamming to live responses.

Mental model

The platform is the body, the network is the nerves, and software plus AI is the brain. You win by updating the brain faster than the enemy can, without asking a foreign vendor for permission.

Aerospace · Gas turbines to scramjets

Jet
Engines

Root question: how do you push an aircraft forward using only the air around it? And why does the answer change with speed?

Based on "The Anatomy of Jet Engines — Gas Turbines to Scramjets" · 20 Jan 2026

The axioms

  1. Thrust = air mass flow × (exhaust speed − flight speed).Every air-breathing engine is a machine for throwing air backward faster than it arrived.
  2. It's more efficient to push a lot of air gently than a little air hard.Propulsive efficiency = 2 / (1 + Vexhaust/Vflight). This one equation explains why airliners have huge fans.
  3. Burning fuel only does useful work if the air is squeezed first.The Brayton cycle: compress, burn, expand. Higher compression means more work extracted from the same fuel.
  4. Speed compresses air for free, and heats it.Slowing air to rest raises its temperature: T₀ = T·(1 + 0.2·M²). At Mach 3 the ram effect alone gives ~37:1 compression. At Mach 7 the air hits ~2,300 K before any fuel burns.
  5. Materials set the ceiling.Turbine gas runs hotter than the blades' own melting point. Only internal cooling channels and ceramic coatings keep them alive. When incoming air gets too hot to cool against, the design has to change.

The trade: gentle acceleration is efficient but gives little thrust per kilogram of air, so you need to move a lot of it. Hence a big fan, with bypass ratios of 5:1 to 12:1 on airliners. Fighters accept lower efficiency for a slim, high-thrust engine plus afterburner.

The difficulty isn't any one part. Every part runs at its limit at the same time, and each part's limit depends on the others. That's why gas turbines are the proving ground: master combustion, rotating machinery, materials and controls here, and those skills carry up the speed ladder.

Breaking the mechanical coupling: turboelectric

In a gas turbine, the turbine is mechanically chained to the compressor, so both must spin at matched speeds. Astro Mechanica breaks that chain. A turbogenerator makes electricity, and electric motors spin the compressor and fan independently. One engine can then shift behaviour across the speed range:

Mode 1Electric fan — ground ops and efficient subsonic flight
Mode 2Combustion + motor-driven compressor — efficient supersonic cruise
Mode 3 · above Mach 3Ramjet-like — forward speed does the compressing

They have hot-fire tested their third-generation engine and raised funding in 2025, including from United Airlines Ventures. The engine can also run on LNG or synthetic methane.

The startup ladder, and where India stands

India's gap, from the holy-grails essay: Kaveri has struggled for decades; fighters rely on GE and Safran, and marine propulsion on Rolls-Royce. Cracking even one engine class is a geopolitical lever for exports and strategic autonomy.

Mental model

Every jet engine answers one question: where does the compression come from? Spinning blades below Mach 2, shock waves from Mach 2 to 4, and at Mach 5+ you stop fighting the air and burn it at full speed.
